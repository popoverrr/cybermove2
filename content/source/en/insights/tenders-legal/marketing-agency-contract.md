# A contract with your marketing agency: what to put in writing so you don't argue about results

Disputes with marketing contractors almost always start the same way: the client expected sales, the agency promised “reach and awareness”, and the contract says “provision of marketing services”. Three months later the two sides understand differently what was done, what was paid for, and who owns the ad accounts, photos and social profiles.
Most of these conflicts can be prevented at the contract stage. A good contract protects both sides: the client knows what they are paying for, and the agency knows exactly what is expected and how its work will be judged. Below is what to put in writing so there is nothing to argue about. This is a practical checklist, not legal advice: have a lawyer review your specific contract.
## What kind of contract it is
In most cases a contract with an agency or a freelance marketer is a contract for paid services. Kazakhstan’s Civil Code (Special Part) devotes a separate chapter to it — Chapter 33, Articles 683–687. A few of its provisions are worth knowing in advance:
- the provider must render the services personally unless the contract provides otherwise (Article 684) — if the agency uses subcontractors, the contract should allow and describe this;
- the customer may withdraw from the contract by paying the provider the expenses actually incurred, while the provider may withdraw only by fully compensating the customer’s losses (Article 686).
If the work produces materials — copy, design, photos, video — copyright questions come in as well; more on that below.
## 1. Scope: not “marketing” but specific work
“Provision of marketing services” does not say what the provider has to do. It is better to describe the scope as a list of work and deliverables, for example in an annex:
- setting up and running ad campaigns in the listed channels;
- producing a set number of creatives per month;
- managing social accounts: number of posts, formats, replying to comments;
- preparing a monthly report in an agreed format;
- building a website or pages with a list of sections and features.
The more specific the list, the easier acceptance and the fewer arguments about whether something was included.
## 2. KPIs and how they are measured
Performance indicators only work if everyone knows in advance how they are calculated. For each indicator, set out:
- what exactly is measured — for example, a qualified lead rather than any enquiry, and what “qualified” means;
- the data source — the client’s CRM, the ad account, the analytics system;
- the period and method — monthly, on a closed period, who produces the report and when;
- what depends on the agency and what doesn’t — the client’s response speed, prices and stock all affect sales but are outside the agency’s control.
A sensible approach is to separate work indicators (plan delivery, campaign quality, cost per lead) from business indicators (sales, revenue), for which the sides agree on targets but assess the contribution together.
## 3. Access, accounts and data
This is the clause behind most conflicts when the relationship ends:
- ad accounts, analytics, social profiles and domains are registered to the client; the agency receives access with the permissions it needs;
- if anything is registered to the agency, the contract sets out its handover at the end of the work — deadline and form;
- the agency undertakes not to delete data — campaigns, audiences, history — and to hand it over on termination;
- the handling of the client’s customer personal data is defined: what is shared, how it is stored, who is responsible.
## 4. Rights to content and materials
If the agency creates copy, design, logos, photos, video or a website, you need to state who owns the rights to the result. Besides the Civil Code, these relations are governed by Kazakhstan’s Law on Copyright and Related Rights. If the contract is silent, the rights may remain with the agency or the individual authors, and using the materials after the work ends becomes risky.
What to include:
- which materials are transferred and in what form — including source files if you need them;
- the scope of rights — full exclusive rights or the right to use in specified ways;
- term and territory of use if the rights are not transferred in full;
- the moment the rights pass — for example, on payment or on signing the acceptance certificate;
- third-party materials — stock images, music, fonts: who buys the licences and in whose name;
- the agency’s warranties that using the materials does not infringe third-party rights.
## 5. Payment: fixed fee, percentage, bonus
Common models:
- a fixed monthly fee for an agreed volume of work;
- a percentage of ad spend — convenient, but it gives the agency an incentive to increase the budget;
- a mixed model — a fixed part plus a bonus for hitting targets.
Separate the agency’s fee from the media budget: who pays the ad platforms, how spend is evidenced, what happens to any unspent balance.
## 6. Acceptance
Acceptance turns “done” into a document. The contract sets out:
- the contents of the period report and its deadline;
- the procedure and deadline for the client to sign the acceptance certificate or send reasoned comments;
- what counts as acceptance if the client does not respond in time;
- how comments are addressed.
Without an acceptance procedure, a dispute about quality becomes a dispute of opinions.
## 7. Confidentiality
The agency sees data on sales, customers, plans and budgets. The confidentiality clause defines which information is protected, how long the obligation lasts after the contract ends, and whether the agency may mention the project in its portfolio — and to what extent.
## 8. Subcontractors and the team
Agencies often bring in freelancers and other contractors for shoots, design or development. Since by law the provider must render the services personally unless the contract says otherwise, bringing in third parties should be explicitly allowed and described:
- whether subcontractors may be used and whether the client must approve them;
- who is liable to the client for their work — the agency, in full;
- how rights to materials created by subcontractors are secured;
- whether the confidentiality obligations extend to them.
If a specific person on the agency side matters to you, that can be written in too — for example, as a requirement to agree on any replacement of a key team member.
## 9. Liability and penalties
Penalties are not a way to make money from the agency; they are a discipline tool for both sides. Reasonable terms:
- a penalty for missing deadlines on specific milestones — a website, a campaign launch, a report;
- liability for losing access or data through the agency’s fault;
- the client’s liability for late payment and for delays in providing materials without which the work cannot proceed;
- a cap on total liability if the parties agree to one.
One-sided terms, where only one party is liable, usually lead the other party to stop keeping its commitments.
## 10. Exit
A good contract provides for a calm parting:
- a notice period for termination — for example, one month;
- handover of materials — accounts, access, source files, reports, and by when;
- settlement — payment for services actually rendered and expenses incurred;
- a transition period — the agency helps hand over to the new team, if agreed.
## After signing
A contract only works if people refer back to it. A few habits that remove most disputes:
- A monthly review against the report: what was done from the scope list, which indicators were reached, what carries over.
- Changes in writing. If the scope changes — a new service, another channel, extra creatives — it is recorded in an addendum, or at least an email confirming it.
- One access register kept by the client: which accounts, profiles and services exist and who has which permissions. It is updated with every change.
- Materials stored with the client. Source files, reports and documents go into a shared company folder, not only onto the agency’s drive.
## Red flags in a contract
- The scope is one line with no list of work.
- Accounts and profiles are created in the agency’s name with no handover clause.
- Nothing is said about rights to materials.
- KPIs are listed without a calculation method or data source.
- There is no acceptance procedure and no deadline for comments.
- Exit is possible only with heavy penalties, or is not described at all.

## FAQ
Can the agency's fee be tied to results? |
Yes, if the result is measurable and depends mainly on the agency. A mixed model is common: a fixed fee for the work and a variable part for hitting targets. What matters is agreeing in advance how each metric is calculated and from which data.
Who should own the ad accounts and social profiles? |
Ideally you, from the start: accounts are created in the company's name and the agency is given access. If they were created in the agency's name, the contract should require them to be handed over on termination.
Can the contract be terminated early? |
Under the Civil Code of Kazakhstan, the customer may withdraw from a contract for paid services by paying the provider the expenses actually incurred. But notice periods, procedure and handover of materials are best set out in the contract so the exit is calm.
Do we need a separate agreement to transfer rights to content? |
Not necessarily separate, but rights must be addressed explicitly: which materials, which rights, for what term and territory, from what moment. Otherwise the rights to the materials may stay with the agency or with the individual authors.
